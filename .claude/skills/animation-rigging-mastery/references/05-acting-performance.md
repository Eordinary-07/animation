# Acting & Performance — Hooks + Illusion of Life + Williams

Sources: Ed Hooks (*Acting for Animators*), Thomas & Johnston (*The Illusion of Life*,
esp. principles, character development, acting/emotions), Williams (*Acting, Dialogue,
Directing*). Use this BEFORE posing any character, and when reviewing whether a shot "reads".

## 1. The 12 principles (Illusion of Life, ch. 3) — essence & reason

1. **Squash & Stretch** — show flexibility and weight; volume constant. Reason: living
   things deform; it also clarifies speed (stretch along travel).
2. **Anticipation** — prepare the audience's eye. Reason: the eye needs to be told where
   to look before the action happens.
3. **Staging** — present the idea so it's unmistakable (pose, camera, light, timing).
   Reason: one clear idea per moment beats ten muddy ones.
4. **Straight-ahead vs Pose-to-pose** — see `03-timing-spacing.md`.
5. **Follow-through & Overlapping action** — parts don't stop together. Reason: physics
   (Whitaker's force-through-joints) and realism of flesh/cloth/hair.
6. **Slow-in & Slow-out** — ease into/out of rest. Reason: inertia.
7. **Arcs** — natural motion travels arcs (mechanical = straight). Reason: biomechanics;
   limbs are levers rotating around joints.
8. **Secondary action** — a supporting action that enriches the main idea (shivering while
   talking cold). Reason: layers of intent make characters real.
9. **Timing** — the mood lives in the timing (fast = energy/comedy, slow = weight/drama).
10. **Exaggeration** — push the idea past realism so it *reads* as realism. Reason: the
    camera and the eye dilute; animation must caricature to communicate.
11. **Solid drawing** — weight, volume, balance in the pose (think 3D even in 2D).
12. **Appeal** — the character is interesting to watch (clear silhouette, contrast, design).
    Not "cute" — readable and compelling.
    Plus IoL's own technique term: the **Moving Hold** — a held pose that stays alive with
    the slightest movement instead of freezing dead; holds are timed decisions, not stops.

## 2. Hooks' acting system (the reason characters act)

### The Seven Essential Acting Concepts (Hooks, ch. 1 — verified verbatim from the book)
> The scanned pages of this chapter are bundled in `references/images/hooks-*.jpg`
> (catalogue: `15-verified-page-images.md`) — open them when teaching or applying these.
1. **Thinking tends to lead to conclusions; emotion tends to lead to action.** Everything
   begins with the brain ("the mind is the pilot" — Walt Disney, Memo to Don Graham 1935:
   *"We think of things before the body does them."*). Emotion is an **automatic value
   response** to thinking. To know what a character feels, first ask what they are
   **thinking** and what their **value system** is.
2. **Acting is reacting. Acting is doing.** (Bill Tytla: "the pose is a reaction to
   something.") First comes a stimulus, then emotion, then action — the reaction precedes
   the doing. Don't *show* results (trembling teeth to show cold) — *act to keep warm*
   (rub hands, stamp feet).
3. **Your character needs to have an objective.** Theatrical movement is purposeful: you
   must always answer "What is he doing?" and the doing must be an action **in pursuit of
   an objective**, 100% of the time. *"Acting is playing an action in pursuit of an
   objective while overcoming an obstacle."* (Chaplin shovels snow → money → food →
   dinner → impress Georgia in *The Gold Rush*.) Take away the intent and the acting dies
   (the Evil Queen stirring the pot = murder; a household cook stirring = cooking).
4. **Play an action until something happens to make you play a different action.** Actions
   are beads on a necklace (Stanislavsky's "beads" — often misheard as "beats"): no gaps,
   one leads to the next. The switch can be external (a human enters the room — *Toy Story*
   toys freeze) or internal (a memory).
5. **All action begins with movement.** Breathing is movement; a thought not expressed in
   action is nothing; "action without movement is impossible" — and movement is a result
   of thinking.
6. **Empathy is the magic key. Audiences empathize with emotion,** not with thinking
   ("empathy" = "feeling into"). The goal of the animator is to **expose emotion through
   the illusion of movement on screen**. The basic theatrical transaction is between actor
   and audience, and the glue is emotion.
7. **A scene is a negotiation.** A theatrical scene requires an obstacle (= conflict) unless
   it is purely connective/expository (Hogarth biking to the diner = connective). Think of
   every scene as a negotiation — negotiations inherently contain conflict.

Core doctrine from the Introduction: animators instinctively play **results** (raise brows
to "show curiosity") — actors play **actions in pursuit of objectives**, and the emotion
shows itself. Never pose an emotion; do the thinking/action and let the emotion ride.

### Hooks chapter doctrines (ch. 2–13 — verified from the bundled opening pages)
Each chapter's opening page is bundled in `references/images/hooks-*.jpg`; section
headings were verified across the whole book. Production translations in *italics*.

- **Ch. 2 The Audience** — the unspoken, iron-clad **actor–audience contract**: the actor
  takes a leadership/high-status position and takes the audience on a journey; the audience
  suspends disbelief. Chekhov: "Everything on the stage must be significant" — carry a
  *feeling of significance* at all times. Animation's contract is play-along non-reality
  (Bugs Bunny defies physics — that's the fun); photo-real work cannot claim both contracts
  at once. Humans also learn/relate by imitation — *every frame must earn its place; stage
  for the audience, not for yourself.*
- **Ch. 3 The Character** — *personality animation*: "The movements and attitudes of a
  figure should display the state of mind of him who makes them, and in such a way that they
  cannot mean anything else" (Leonardo da Vinci). Culhane: personality first, second and
  third; Chuck Jones: "Personality. That is the key, the drum, the fife. Forget the plot."
  But personality is NOT design trivia (a paunch, a squinty eye) — build a **character bible**
  with Hooks' *Human Character Analysis* (p. 22): male or female? age? physical health?
  appearance? hygiene? intelligence? diet? culture? history? religion? income? occupation?
  education? sexual orientation? sense of humor? family? friends? **inner rhythm?**
  psychology (introvert/extrovert)? goals and dreams? name? (Then *Primal Analysis* reduces
  it to the animal core.) *Fill this in during the Pipeline Brief (ref 14) — it drives walk,
  gesture, timing and facial choices.*
- **Ch. 4 The Scene** — every scene is a **negotiation** (obstacle = conflict, and conflict
  need not be negative: blueberry pie vs chocolate cheesecake). Unless purely expository,
  conflict must be present at all times; only three kinds exist: **(1) with another
  character, (2) with himself, (3) with his situation.** Then *Status Negotiations* (who is
  up/down, moment to moment), *Scenes Begin in the Middle* (enter late), *The Rehearsal*
  (play it out before keying), *Emotion and Empathy* (incl. Paul Ekman's expression research),
  *Acting Is a Process of Exposing, Not Hiding*, and **adrenaline moments** (see ch. 12).
- **Ch. 5 Movement** — "Animation is movement. Movement is animation." The senses rank
  sight > hearing > smell > touch > taste (Hall, *The Hidden Dimension*: the eyes are "as
  much as a thousand times as effective as the ears") — **body language transmits a more
  powerful message than dialogue**. *Animating Force Versus Form*: animate the force/intent,
  not the outline. **Power Centers**: at ease the power center sits in the chest leading you
  forward; drop it to the stomach (pregnant walk), to the feet (shuffling drunk), behind the
  neck (pushes you) — *"Every character has a power center"* (choose it per character; it
  drives the root/COG pattern). *The Psychological Gesture* (one gesture that embodies the
  intent), *Pantomime*, and **Laban Movement Theory**: effort = Space (direct/indirect) ×
  Time (sustained/sudden) × Weight (strong/light) [+ Flow] → the **8 basic effort actions** —
  Press (direct, sustained, strong), Wring (indirect, sustained, strong), Glide (direct,
  sustained, light), Float (indirect, sustained, light), Thrust (direct, sudden, strong),
  Slash (indirect, sudden, strong), Dab (direct, sudden, light), Flick (indirect, sudden,
  light). A character's "weight" is the tension between gravity and upward lift (relaxation
  = weight; exuberance pushes away from gravity). *Use Laban to set spacing/ease curves per
  action verb (ref 03), and note alcohol/drugs change the effort signature.*
- **Ch. 6 Speech** — "Acting has very little to do with words": the iceberg is 15% words
  above the water, 85% underneath — but you must still build the unseen 85%. **Movement
  precedes words**: when a thought occurs, head/eye/shoulder/neck move first; in the instant
  before a character speaks, he moves, expressing the impulse — the body runs **a beat ahead
  of the mouth**. To prerecorded dialogue, work backward from the voice to the impulse (find
  the most awkward word, say it aloud, feel where it lives in your body). Plus *Active
  Listening*: the listener is acting too. *(Pairs with Osipa: thought before sound, ref 06.)*
- **Ch. 7 The Camera** — "the reaction shot carries 90 percent of the baggage": editors cut
  to reactions; actors work **listen → react → speak**. Animation edits beforehand (the
  storyboard is the edit). Performance scale follows shot size: master/wide = broad and
  physical; **the closer the camera, the more still the performance** — in extreme close-up
  everything is in the eyes. *The Actor Generally Leads the Camera* (performance first,
  camera follows). *(Shot-size → gesture-size table in ref 08.)*
- **Ch. 8 Technique** — "Technique is whatever works"; **don't chase your audience** (pushing
  harder loses them — reconnect with intention instead). **Simplify Your Actions**: "The more
  direct, simple, and specific your acting choices are, the better" — play "I want you to go
  on a date with me," not the general need "I want to be loved." Also: *Avoid Ambivalence*;
  *Never Deny the Reality of Your Scene Partner*; *What Kind of Animal Would Your Character
  Be?*; *Live Action Reference*, *Rotoscoping* and *Mocap* (reference is a tool, not a
  crutch); *Character Rhythm*; *Mirrors* — the animator, like Diderot's actor, keeps a
  controlling distance from the performance rather than drowning in it.
- **Ch. 9 The Form** — "Lift the hood of any good comedy, and you'll find the engine of a
  good drama": **comedy is drama exaggerated, heightened and enriched** — a spin-off of
  tragedy, never a stand-alone form (Noel Coward: "Comedy is hard"). Don't analyze "funny
  movement"; build real drama and heighten it (farce, caricature). *Comedy shots still need
  objectives, obstacles and stakes.*
- **Ch. 10 The Medium** — acting calibration is medium-specific: video games (the player
  leans in — interactive, loopable, readable at gameplay distance), television commercials
  (implied visual message; pictures not words; playing to the camera), features vs TV.
  *Decide the medium in the Pipeline Brief; it sets performance scale and cycle design.*
- **Ch. 11 Classroom Exercises** — improv games are the acting gym ("not about being funny —
  about paying attention to your own processes"): Power Center Games (move the center like a
  balloon — light/heavy, push/pull — and let it initiate the walk), Animal Exercises, What Is
  My Profession/Age?, the Given Circumstances Game, the Gibberish Exercise, Boss and Workers.
  *Steal these as thumbnailing/warm-up methods before blocking (ref 09).*
- **Ch. 12 The Iron Giant: An Acting Analysis** — scene-by-scene masterclass; the
  **adrenaline-moment test**: "ask yourself if it is likely that the characters in the scene
  will remember the scene when they are in their old age." If yes, play it at full voltage.
  Empathy is engineered with identifiable techniques, scene after scene.
- **Ch. 13 Postscript: What Is Method Acting?** — the old school *posed* emotions from books
  of stock expressions; Stanislavsky (Moscow Arts Theatre, 1897) replaced indicating with
  emotional triggers (memories, smells, sights) — *feel the moment first; the pose is the
  by-product, never the starting point.*

### Script analysis before animation (Hooks/Stanislavsky framework — objective, obstacle and negotiation verified in ch. 1 & 4)
- **Given circumstances**: what's true right now (where, when, who, what just happened).
- **Objective**: what does the character want FROM THE OTHER CHARACTER right now?
  (Always about the other person — "to convince", "to escape", "to comfort".)
- **Obstacle**: what's in the way (external or internal). Without an obstacle there is no
  negotiation — only exposition.
- **Tactics**: the VERBS they use to pursue the objective (beg, threaten, charm, seduce,
  lecture, ignore). **Animate tactics, not words.**
- **The moment before**: the character exists before the first frame — enter with energy
  already in motion. Reason: starting from zero reads as puppetry.

### Thinking vs feeling
- The face shows *feeling*; the body shows *thinking* (intent, attention).
- What a character *does* reveals what they *feel* — movement provokes emotion
  (change your posture and your mood follows; animators work the body to sell the mind).
- Stage vs screen: screen acting is smaller. In animation: the camera distance decides
  the size of the gesture (play to the shot).

### Gesture & body language (the body cannot lie)
- Open vs closed postures; weight shifts = confidence shifts; feet point where attention is.
- Hands: the second face. Keep hand poses simple and asymmetric (see twinning below).
- **Contrast** (Williams echoes this): look for the contrast in attitude, rhythm, size —
  contrast reads as thought. A character that's the same everywhere reads as "no thought".

### Scene work
- Beats = changes in tactic. A scene is a series of turns: status shifts, escalation, result.
- **An acting point** (Williams): the one moment the shot exists for — land it with timing
  and contrast. "Change of expression" happens on the acting point, not continuously.
- Comedy: surprise + contrast + timing. Anticipate the audience's expectation, then break it.

### Dialogue (Hooks + Williams "Dialogue")
- People speak to get what they want — the line is a tactic, the *subtext* is the action.
- **Phrasing** (Williams): break the line into phrases; body accents follow phrase accents
  (1-2 frames late is natural).
- **Picture and sound sync**: lips hit the sound; body SUPPORTS the meaning — don't animate
  every syllable in the body.
- **Attitude** (Williams "The Secret"): act the line yourself, out loud, before keying.

## 3. Williams' acting toolkit (chapter "Acting")

- **Change of expression** at the acting point — fewer, clearer changes beat constant motion.
- **Symmetry / twinning**: NEVER mirror left/right (arms, brows, hips). Symmetry = death.
  Break it by offset, scale, angle, or timing.
- **Eyes** lead thought: the audience reads the eyes first — they arrive before the head,
  leave after; saccades and blinks mark thought changes (see `06-facial-lip-sync.md`).
- **Steal it!** — act it out, film yourself, use reference. Reason: invented motion betrays
  itself; studied motion reads as life.
- **Body language** and profile "instant read" silhouettes.

## 4. Directing notes (Williams "Directing")

- The brief: know the intent of the shot before animating. Leica reel (animatic) sets
  timing context — respect the cut and the shot's duration.
- Separate the characters: each has their own objective; "best foot forward" (start with
  the character's most readable pose); voice recording drives phrasing; hook-ups connect
  shots (continuous action across cuts); research and reference every time.

## 5. Blender workflow for performance

1. Write the objective/obstacle/tactic on a sticky note in the file (Text datablock).
2. Shoot/find reference; note the acting points and beats on the timeline (markers).
3. Block poses-to-pose: extreme attitude poses ONLY at beats (Stepped interpolation).
4. Check silhouettes in camera at each beat (solid drawing + staging).
5. Breakdowns carry the *tactic change* between beats.
6. Add eyes/lips after the body acts (the body is the performance; the face is the detail).
7. Polish: offsets, arcs, accents; review at speed with sound.
