# 23 — Directing the Screen (Francis Glebas · Bruce Block · Steven Katz · Walter Murch)

> Where ref 18 finds the *intention*, this file makes it **visible**: visual structure,
> audience attention, staging/camera grammar, and the cut. Sources: *Directing the
> Story* (Francis Glebas, Disney story artist), *The Visual Story* 2nd ed. (Bruce Block), *Film
> Directing Shot by Shot* (Steven D. Steven Katz) — all verified from the books' own text/TOCs —
> plus Walter Murch's Rule of Six **as stated in the uploaded summary edition** of *In the
> Blink of an Eye* (the full book is a pending upgrade; use at that level).

## 1. Bruce Block: visual structure (the image has a structure, like the story does)

**"The basic visual components are space, line, shape, tone, color, movement, and
rhythm."** Every frame you stage is built from exactly these seven — direct them on
purpose or they direct themselves:
- **Space** (deep vs flat, frontal vs diagonal volume), **Line & Shape** (the directional
  skeleton — where the eye travels), **Tone** (brightness architecture), **Color**
  (palette discipline), **Movement** (object/camera), **Rhythm** (repetition patterns
  in all of the above).
- **The master control is Contrast and Affinity**: *"What is contrast? Contrast means
  difference."* For each component, difference (contrast) creates intensity and energy;
  similarity (affinity) creates calm and unity. A chase builds because every component
  increases contrast toward the climax (his analyses trace exactly this build).
- **Ch. 9, Story and Visual Structure**: the visual components are chosen to parallel
  the story structure — rising story intensity = rising visual contrast. Practical law
  for Blender: when ref 18's emotional arc rises, raise contrast in at least two
  components (e.g., tone + movement, or color + space); when it resolves, return toward
  affinity. A climax staged with opening-scene visuals will feel flat no matter how
  good the animation is.
- Ch. 10 is titled **"Practice, Not Theory"** — the components are a rehearsal
  vocabulary: name them in the Pipeline Brief's shot intentions ("S7: deep space, tone
  contrast high, rhythm staccato") and judge renders against those words (ref 19 Gate L).

## 2. Francis Glebas: directing = directing attention

- **"What Do Directors Direct?" (ch. 6)** — attention. Get it, keep it, and keep the
  structure invisible while doing so ("Keeping Structure Invisible: Tricks of
  Attention"; the director as magician; Gestalt grouping does the heavy lifting).
  Blender translation: one focal hierarchy per frame (ref 18 §2.6) — brightest, sharpest,
  most contrast, most movement = where the eye goes; give the story point all four and
  deny them to everything else.
- **Images ask questions; stories are hierarchies of narrative questions** ("How Images
  Ask Questions", "Hierarchy of Narrative Questions"): every shot should pose or answer
  a question (*will she open the door? what's behind him?*). *"Nondramatic ones have
  known outcomes"* — a shot whose outcome is already known is dead air. In the beat map
  (ref 18 §2), write the open question per shot; cuts land where answers arrive.
- **Structure at three zoom levels** (ch. 5 "Tactics to Reach the Goal", ch. 12 "The BIG
  Picture: Story Structures" — Propp's functions, the hero's journey, turning points,
  types of scenes) and **"Aiming for the Heart" (ch. 13)**: fears/flaws/wants/needs,
  the Rubberband Theory of Comedy (stretch expectation, snap release), emotional truth
  over literal truth. This is ref 18's intention engine with a storyboard artist's
  toolbox — including, literally, a chapter-section on **Walt Stanchfield's gesture class**,
  caricature, and "Drawing for Clarity and the Use of Clear Silhouettes" (ref 24).
- Storyboard discipline (his craft): draw the film first at thumbnail scale; a board
  panel, like a Walt Stanchfield pose, must read its story point in one glance. In Blender:
  Gate L layout stills ARE the board — judge them as panels (ref 19).

## 3. Steven Katz: staging & camera grammar (the continuity style)

Shot by Shot's architecture is a complete grammar; use it as the checklist behind ref 08:
- **Spatial connections** (composing shots so cuts preserve geography) + **temporal
  connections** (editing) — continuity is designed in staging, not rescued in the edit.
- **Staging dialogue** as named patterns (two subjects, three subjects, four-plus,
  mobile staging, depth of the frame): who moves, who is still, who owns the foreground
  = who owns the scene. In Blender, block these patterns at Gate L before any facial
  work (ref 19) — power shifts are staged with bodies and camera, not eyebrows.
- **Open vs closed framings** (does the frame feel like a window on a bigger world or a
  sealed composition?) and **point of view** as deliberate choices, not defaults.
- **The moving camera** (Pan, Crane Shot, Tracking Shot, Tracking Shot Choreography):
  a move is choreography between camera and subject — motivated by a story beat, timed
  to it, and rehearsed as one action. Unmotivated drift is the camera equivalent of
  twinning (ref 13): it reads as nobody directing.
- **Transitions & format** chosen for meaning (a cut asserts, a dissolve remembers,
  a fade closes a chapter) — declare in the brief; never let the editor-brain default.

## 4. Walter Murch: the cut (Rule of Six — summary-level provenance)

The uploaded *Blink of an Eye* copy is a chapter-summary edition; its Rule of Six
table states the hierarchy for evaluating any cut, in priority order: **Emotion**
(evoke intended feelings) → **Story** (advance the narrative) → **Rhythm** (natural
timing and flow) → **Eye-trace** (maintain the audience's focus within the frame) →
**Planarity** (respect the 2D representation of 3D action) → **Three-dimensional
continuity** (spatial relationships — "though least critical"). Its stated overall
argument: *"If a cut enhances emotion and story, minor rhythm or continuity issues are
acceptable."* Use it at shot-join review (Gate P continuity check, ref 19): when a join
feels wrong, diagnose top-down — an emotion-breaking cut cannot be rescued by perfect
screen-direction. (Full-text verification pending a complete edition upload.)

## 5. The directing checklist (per shot, 60 seconds)

1. One idea + one open narrative question (refs 18, Francis Glebas)? 2. Focal hierarchy: does the
story point own brightness/contrast/movement? 3. Which visual components carry the
contrast at this point of the arc (Bruce Block)? 4. Staging pattern named; geography survives
the neighboring cuts (Steven Katz)? 5. Camera move motivated by a beat, choreographed with the
subject? 6. The cut in/out passes the Rule of Six top-down? Fail any → fix at layout,
where it costs minutes — not at polish, where it costs days.
