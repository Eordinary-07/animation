# 18 — The Script Intention Engine (intention first, technique second)

> **The law of this file: never animate a line of script until you can state its
> intention.** Technique is cheap; intention is what separates a AAA studio from a
> button-pusher. This file systematizes verified doctrines already in this skill —
> Hooks' Seven Concepts and objective/obstacle acting (ref 05), Williams' Acting Point,
> anti-twinning and directing (refs 03–05, `images/`), Whitaker's timing reads (ref 03),
> Jeremy Birn's and James Gurney's light mood (ref 16 §6) — into one protocol that works for **any**
> script: a feature scene, a 6-word instruction, a product shot, a game cutscene.

## 1. The five questions (answer before opening Blender)

For every piece, write down — literally, in the Pipeline Brief (ref 14 §E):
1. **Objective** — what does the main character *want* in this piece? (a verb: to win, to
   hide, to be forgiven…)
2. **Obstacle** — what blocks them? (another will, the world, themselves — Hooks' three
   conflicts)
3. **Tactic** — how do they pursue it *right now*? (charm, threaten, perform, retreat…)
4. **Audience feeling** — what must the viewer FEEL at the end of this beat? (not "know" —
   feel)
5. **The one idea** — each shot gets exactly one job, stated as: *"This shot exists so the
   audience feels ___ when ___."* A shot with two ideas has zero.

If the script doesn't state these, infer them professionally (section 6) — never proceed
without them. Animation without intention defaults to generic motion, and generic motion
is the #1 amateur tell (ref 13).

## 2. The extraction protocol (any input → production-ready understanding)

1. **Logline it.** One sentence: *[who] wants [what] but [obstacle]*. If you can't, the
   request needs one clarifying question (section 6) — about intention, never mechanics.
2. **Beat map.** Break the piece into beats: a beat ends when the character's tactic
   changes or the emotional value flips (+→−). Number them B1, B2, B3… Each beat gets:
   objective / tactic / feeling / estimated seconds.
3. **Character bibles, one paragraph each** (Hooks): who they are, their power center,
   how they think, what they'd never do. Two characters pursuing the same objective must
   move *differently* — the bible is why.
4. **Emotional arc.** Sketch intensity over time across beats. Peaks need valleys before
   them (contrast is what an audience feels); flat arcs = flat film, no matter the polish.
5. **Shot intentions.** Convert beats to shots; one idea per shot (some beats need 2–3
   shots, some shots serve one beat beat-for-beat). This list drives ref 14's shot list.
6. **Readability hierarchy per shot.** What must read in the first half-second, in
   silhouette, at thumbnail size? That element gets the staging, the light, the detail
   budget (Owen Demers' detail-contrast rule, ref 17 §9).
7. **Subtext pass.** Where does the character say one thing and want another? Mark those
   beats: they get counter-action, asymmetry, and reaction beats (ref 05) — subtext is
   where AAA acting lives.

## 3. Intention → technique (the translation tables)

**Feeling → timing & spacing** (Williams/Whitaker, refs 03–04):
- *Menace/dread* → slow, even approach; long holds; sudden stops; minimal blinks.
- *Comedy* → contrast: long dumbfounded holds vs 2–4 frame snaps; the laugh lives in the
  hold AFTER the action (reaction timing).
- *Tenderness* → slow-in/slow-out everywhere; small amplitudes; overlap prominent; breath.
- *Panic* → dense spacing, broken rhythm, incomplete gestures (starts that abort).
- *Power/confidence* → economy: fewer, larger, committed moves; long moving holds;
  others move around the powerful one.

**Feeling → body** (Hooks/Williams, refs 04–05):
- Power center placement (chest = pride, head = intellect, pelvis = earthiness) leads
  every move; Laban effort (weight/time/space/flow) is chosen per beat, not per character.
- Asymmetry always; twinning reads as lying or robotic (Williams, verified page).
- The Acting Point: each beat change is punctuated by ONE clear physical punctuation.

**Feeling → camera** (ref 08; Jeremy Birn's five qualities, ref 16 §6):
- Intimacy → closer, longer lens, still or breathing camera. Spectacle → wide, moving.
- Instability → subtle handheld/dutch; stability/control → locked or gliding.
- Who owns the shot = who the camera favors in size/angle; power shifts get angle shifts.

**Feeling → light & surface** (refs 16–17): hardness of light (James Gurney), color contrast,
and detail density follow the emotional arc — the climax beat earns the most contrast,
the quiet beat the softest. Surfacing wear tells the object's story the same way.

## 4. Format adaptability (what changes by type of piece)

| Format | What changes |
|---|---|
| **Dialogue scene** | Acting beats drive everything; camera serves eyelines; lip-sync ±1 frame (ref 06); reaction shots ≥ action shots |
| **Action** | Readability at speed: extreme pose contrast, clear silhouettes, staging geography first; chaos is composed, not random |
| **Comedy gag** | Setup must be over-clear; timing contrast; hold for the laugh; never animate through the punchline |
| **Horror/tension** | Withholding: stillness, off-screen space, slow reveals; sound-shaped timing |
| **Musical/dance** | Beat grid locks timing; accents on musical hits; camera moves on phrase boundaries |
| **Creature/non-human** | Bible from anatomy + one human-readable intention channel (eyes/posture); weight is the acting |
| **Product/hero object** | The object is the character: its "objective" is the brand feeling; surface/light do the acting (refs 16–17) |
| **Montage/sequence** | Each shot = one image-idea; rhythm of cuts carries the arc; individual shots under-acted, sequence over-delivers |

## 5. The realism dial (style is a setting, not an accident)

Declare the style target in the brief, then scale consistently:
- **Realistic (filmic CG)** — true physics, true timing, subtle overlap, measured PBR
  values, micro-movement over gesture.
- **Stylized (feature animation)** — anticipation/follow-through ×1.5, squash ≤ ~15%,
  holds longer, spacing snappier, caricatured silhouettes.
- **Cartoon** — timing on contrasts (holds vs 2-frame moves), smears/multiples allowed,
  physics negotiable *in service of the gag*, poses extreme.
Mixing dials inside one piece without story reason = instant amateur read (ref 13).

## 6. When the script is thin (adaptability rules)

- **Ask the user only when intention genuinely branches** ("is she angry at him, or
  protecting him?") — one question, with your recommended default.
- **Never ask about mechanics** (fps, rig type, map sizes) you can decide professionally
  from refs 14/16/17. Decide, state the choice in the brief, proceed.
- **Defaults that protect intention:** if tone is unstated, play it truthful before funny;
  if duration is unstated, budget beats at natural performance length and say so; if a
  character trait is unstated, derive it from their actions in the script (people are
  what they do — Thomas & Johnston).
- **A 6-word instruction still gets the five questions.** "A robot finds a flower" →
  objective: understand this fragile living thing; obstacle: its own clumsy strength;
  feeling: wonder → tenderness; every technique choice follows.

## 7. Mini worked example

Instruction: *"An old guard lets a kid sneak past."*
- Logline: A guard who misses his own youth lets a trespassing kid escape.
- Beats: B1 duty (spots kid — tactic: intimidate) → B2 recognition (kid's fear = his
  memory; value flips) → B3 mercy disguised as duty (tactic: perform not-seeing).
- Shots: S1 wide geography (idea: the rule of this place) · S2 guard CU, beat change ON
  his eyes (idea: he remembers) · S3 the turn-away — his deliberate "inspection" of the
  opposite wall while kid crosses behind (idea: mercy in uniform).
- Technique follows: S3 guard gets exaggerated officious business (subtext!), kid's
  crossing in near-silhouette; the hold after kid exits sells the feeling. *That* is
  intention-first — the same script animated as "guard stands, kid runs" is nothing.

## 8. Robert McKee deep grammar (Story — verified from the book's pages)

Robert McKee's architecture (Contents verified, `images/dir-02-mckee-contents.jpg`): **Part 1**
The Writer and the Art of Story · **Part 2** The Elements of Story (structure spectrum,
setting, genre, character, meaning) · **Part 3** The Principles of Story Design (the
substance of story, **the inciting incident**, act design, **scene design**, scene
analysis, composition, crisis/climax/resolution) · **Part 4** The Writer at Work (the
principle of antagonism, exposition, problems and solutions, character).

- **His ground rules** (Introduction, read verbatim): *"Story is about principles, not
  rules… about eternal, universal forms, not formulas… about archetypes, not
  stereotypes… about thoroughness, not shortcuts."* And the animator's commandment:
  *"Story is about respect, not disdain, for the audience"* — in a darkened theatre the
  audience's *"collective IQ jumps twenty-five points"*; they see your ending coming
  before you do. Never animate down to them.
- **The Gap — how scenes turn** (Ch. 10 Scene Design, p.236 verified,
  `images/dir-03-mckee-scene-design.jpg`): a scene turns when reality splits from
  expectation: *"The moment we see that a thirty-two-year-old man can't make breakfast
  the scene turns. The question 'Why?' sends us back through the few minutes of film
  that precede the gap."* Darth Darth Vader's *"I'm your father"* is his giant example — the
  gap *"hurls the audience back"* to re-read everything before it, with new meaning.
  **Intention-engine diagnostic:** for every beat in the beat map (§2), name the
  expectation and the result. If they're the same, there is no gap, the scene does not
  turn, and no amount of beautiful motion will make it dramatic — redesign the beat
  (stronger obstacle, per his Principle of Antagonism, Part 4) before animating it.
- Use the chapter map as the deep reference when a script needs structural diagnosis:
  weak opening → Ch. 8 Inciting Incident; sagging middle → Ch. 9 Act Design + Ch. 12
  Composition; flat scenes → Ch. 10–11; endings → Ch. 13 Crisis, Climax, Resolution.
